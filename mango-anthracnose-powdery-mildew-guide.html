Mango Anthracnose & Powdery Mildew: Protecting Flowers and Fruit

Compiled by the Crop Care AI editorial team from public ICAR and state agricultural university advisories • Category: Fruit Crop Pathology • 7 min read

Two fungal diseases cause the biggest losses in mango flowering and fruiting: Powdery Mildew (Oidium mangiferae) on panicles, and Anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides) on flowers, young fruits and ripening fruits. Timing of control is more important than the product, because a panicle that is badly infected cannot be saved.

1. Powdery mildew

  • Symptoms: a white powdery coating on panicles, flowers and tiny fruits, followed by flower drop and fruit shedding.
  • Favouring weather: cool nights, cloudy and humid days during panicle emergence and flowering.

2. Anthracnose

  • Blossom blight: black patches on flowers and panicle stalks, and panicles drying out.
  • Fruit symptoms: dark, sunken spots on young and ripening fruits that enlarge during storage and transport.
  • Spread: rain splash and humid weather spread spores; old infected twigs and leaves carry the fungus over.

3. Cultural measures

  • Pruning and sanitation: prune dense branches after harvest to improve airflow, and remove dried twigs and diseased material.
  • Avoid overhead wetting: high humidity inside the canopy increases both diseases.
  • Balanced nutrition: avoid excess nitrogen that pushes soft, susceptible growth.

4. Commonly used sprays

Commonly used sprays (per Litre of water)

Spray at panicle emergence, repeat after 15 days if humid weather continues, and avoid spraying in the middle of full bloom when pollinating insects are most active. Alternate between groups.

  • Wettable sulphur 80% WP: 3 g per Litre for powdery mildew.
  • Hexaconazole 5% EC: 1 ml per Litre for powdery mildew.
  • Carbendazim 50% WP: 1 g per Litre for anthracnose.
  • Mancozeb 75% WP: 2.5 g per Litre (protective) for anthracnose.
  • Copper oxychloride 50% WP: 3 g per Litre for anthracnose.

5. After harvest

Harvest with a short stalk, avoid bruising, and handle fruit gently. A hot-water dip (about 52 degrees C for 10 minutes) is a recognised way to reduce anthracnose rot in storage. Follow guidance from your horticulture department or KVK on the correct temperature and time, because mistakes can scald the fruit.
